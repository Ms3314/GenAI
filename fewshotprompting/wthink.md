Great goal. You’re in 3rd year CSE—six months is enough to build a strong, interview-ready profile and rack up several internship opportunities. There’s no hard “guarantee,” but if you follow this disciplined plan, your odds will be substantially higher.

First, a quick mindset note: be prepared to hustle. Internships come from a mix of solid projects, strong interview prep, and good networking. Consistency beats last-minute bursts.

Six-month action plan (month by month)

Month 1: Set up a strong foundation
- Define your target track: web development, data science, ML, cybersecurity, systems, etc. Pick 1–2 focuses to avoid spreading yourself too thin.
- Update your resume to 1 page. Create a “Projects” section with 2–3 concrete items: problem, tech stack, your role, and measurable impact (e.g., reduced load time by 40%, processed 10k records/day).
- Clean up your GitHub profile: pin 3 notable repos; ensure READMEs clearly explain the project and your contributions.
- Build a simple personal portfolio (optional but powerful): 1–2 projects + a short bio + contact method.
- Optimize LinkedIn: complete profile, headline with your track, a couple of project posts, and a clear call-to-action (e.g., “Seeking 2025 summer internship in Backend/DSA”).
- Start a mindset habit: set a weekly time block for interview prep and coding practice.

Month 2: Solid projects + start networking
- Create 2 solid in-depth projects that showcase your track. Examples:
  - Web: a responsive app with a real API and authentication.
  - Data/ML: a small model with data import, cleaning, evaluation, and deployment (even if on Colab).
- Contribute to at least one open-source project or join a hackathon. Document your contributions in your portfolio.
- Begin targeted outreach: reach out to 5–10 alumni or professionals per week on LinkedIn for quick informational chats. Have a concise pitch: who you are, what you’re studying, what you’re looking for, and how you can add value.
- Gather feedback on your resume and projects from mentors, seniors, or career center staff.

Month 3: Interview prep basics + apply
- Start structured DSA/Problem-Solving practice. 60–90 minutes daily:
  - 1–2 easy-medium problems each day, focus on patterns (arrays, strings, trees, graphs, DP, hashing).
  - Learn 2–3 core data structures thoroughly (arrays, linked lists, stacks/queues, trees, hash maps).
  - Do a weekly mock interview with a friend or use a platform (Pramp, Interviewing.io) to build comfort with coding interviews.
- Behavioral prep: craft STAR stories for typical questions (team conflict, failure, leadership, project impact). Practice aloud.
- Begin targeted applications: aim for 15–25 tailored applications per week. Attach a tailored resume version for each role (highlight relevant projects/skills).

Month 4: Apply aggressively + referrals + mock interviews
- Push applications to target companies + startups. Prioritize roles aligned with your track.
- Leverage referrals: ask alumni, professors, or mentors for introductions to team members. A referral often improves chances more than a generic application.
- Continue interview prep: simulate 2–3 mock interviews per week; add system-design basics if you’re aiming for mid-to-senior levels (even for internships, some firms test it).
- Sharpen your portfolio and projects based on feedback. Ensure your write-ups clearly explain:
  - Problem statement
  - Your approach and tech stack
  - Data/metrics demonstrating impact
  - Any collaborative work and your specific contributions

Month 5: Refine, negotiate, and expand
- If you’ve started interviews, log questions you struggled with and revise. Add new practice problems targeting your weak areas.
- Expand your network: request short 15–20 minute calls with people in your target teams. Ask insightful questions about what the team values and what projects they’re excited about.
- Prepare for different interview formats: coding, take-home assignments, and portfolio reviews. Have a plan for each (e.g., take-home: outline, timebox, and a clean submission with tests).
- Apply to additional openings, including remote opportunities and internships in other regions if you’re open to relocation or remote work.

Month 6: Lock in opportunities and finalize readiness
- Accept offers carefully if you’re lucky; if not, you should still have several solid options due to your portfolio and network.
- Final prep: rehearse your pitch, refine your resume for specific roles, and practice whiteboard or online coding interviews.
- Build a small “winning” project you can showcase in interviews, ideally one that demonstrates end-to-end work (design → implementation → testing → deployment or demo).
- Have a fallback plan: freelancer micro-projects, paid internships, or research assistant roles if available.

Weekly cadence you can follow
- Monday–Friday: 1–2 hours of coding practice + 1–2 hours of project work; 30–60 minutes networking (outreach to alumni, mentors, or potential teammates).
- Saturday: 2–3 hours interview prep (mock interviews or problem-solving practice) + project progress review.
- Sunday: rest or light review; update resume, GitHub, LinkedIn with any new achievements.

Resume, portfolio, and interview-ready tips
- Resume bullets should be impact-focused: action verb + project + tech stack + quantified result.
- Projects: include a short problem statement, your approach, the tech stack, and the impact. Include links to GitHub and live demos if possible.
- LinkedIn/GitHub: keep content consistent; add brief project summaries and a pinned repo with your best work.
- Interview prep: use a structured approach (explain your thought process clearly, verbalize why you choose certain data structures, and discuss time/space complexity).

What to avoid
- Do not rely on one big hack or a single “perfect” project. A few well-executed projects plus steady interview prep beats a single flashy but shallow effort.
- Do not ignore networking. Most roles come through referrals or recruiter outreach; passive applications have lower yield.
- Do not wait for a perfect resume or perfect project. Iterate quickly; you can improve while you apply.
